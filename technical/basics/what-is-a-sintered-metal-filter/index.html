What Is a Sintered Metal Filter?

2026-09-01  · 3 min read

A sintered metal filter is a filter element made from metal powder that has been bonded into a rigid, porous body. Instead of weaving wires or laying down fibers, the manufacturer compacts fine metal powder into a mold and heats it — a process called sintering — until the particles weld together at their contact points. The spaces between the particles remain open, forming a continuous network of pores that lets fluid pass while retaining contamination.

The result is a filter that is simultaneously the filter medium and its own support structure: one monolithic piece of porous metal with no separate layers to delaminate or migrate.

The Basic Structure

Zoom into a sintered metal filter and you see packed powder particles fused at their contact points. Three features define its behavior:

  • Particles — typically stainless steel or titanium powder, selected by particle size distribution. Particle size strongly influences pore size: finer powder generally produces finer pores.
  • Bonds — the sintered necks where particles have welded together. These give the body its mechanical strength and rigidity.
  • Pores — the open, interconnected voids between particles. Fluid flows through this maze of channels, and particles larger than the pore constrictions are captured throughout the element wall, not just on its surface.

Because the pore network is three-dimensional, sintered metal filters act as depth filters: dirt is held inside the full thickness of the porous wall, which gives them substantial dirt-holding capacity for a rigid medium.

How Filtration Happens

In service, the process fluid flows through the porous wall under a pressure difference. As the fluid turns through the tortuous pore paths, particles are retained by a combination of direct interception (too large to enter a pore opening), inertial impaction, and, for very fine particles, diffusion-driven capture on pore surfaces. Over time the retained particles build a filter cake on and within the medium, and the pressure drop across the element rises — the standard signal that cleaning or replacement is due.

This retention behavior is why sintered metal filters are usually specified with a filtration rating (for example, a nominal or absolute rating in micrometers) rather than with a single pore diameter: real retention is a statistical property of the whole pore network. We cover this in detail in the filtration performance section of this knowledge center.

Why Engineers Choose Sintered Metal Filters

The sintered powder structure delivers a characteristic set of advantages:

  1. Mechanical robustness. The element is a load-bearing metal part. It tolerates vibration, pressure pulses, thermal cycling, and handling that would damage pleated or fiber media.
  2. High-temperature capability. Being all-metal, these filters operate at temperatures far beyond polymer media — the practical limit depends on the alloy and the atmosphere.
  3. Chemical compatibility. Stainless steel covers a broad range of industrial fluids; titanium extends this into strongly corrosive, chloride-bearing duties.
  4. Cleanability and reuse. The rigid pores can be backflushed, ultrasonically cleaned, or chemically regenerated, so elements are often reused many times rather than discarded.
  5. Stable geometry. Pore structure does not relax, swell, or channel the way flexible media can, so performance stays consistent over the element’s life.

Common Forms

Sintered metal filters are produced in several standardized forms:

  • Filter elements — tubular or cylindrical cartridges, the workhorse for process streams.
  • Filter discs and plates — flat porous discs for compact housings and instruments.
  • Spargers — porous elements run in reverse: gas is pushed through the wall to disperse into a liquid as fine bubbles.

All three forms share the same powder-sintered construction, and all are available on this site in stainless steel and titanium. See our product overview for the full range.

Where They Are Used

Typical duties include gas and liquid filtration in chemical processing, catalyst retention, steam filtration, pharmaceutical and food processes, polymer handling, and laboratory equipment. The deciding factors are usually temperature, chemistry, cleanliness requirements, and the economics of a reusable element versus disposable media.

The Bottom Line

A sintered metal filter is best understood as engineered porosity: metal powder turned into a rigid, porous machine component that filters, supports itself, and survives conditions that destroy conventional filter media. If your process runs hot, aggressive, or simply demands a filter that can be regenerated rather than replaced, sintered metal is the technology to evaluate.
