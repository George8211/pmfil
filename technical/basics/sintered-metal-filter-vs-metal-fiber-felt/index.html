Sintered Metal Filter vs Metal Fiber Felt

2026-09-09  · 4 min read

Metal fiber felt and powder sintered metal are the two main depth-type all-metal filter media, and they compete for many of the same high-temperature, high-strength duties. Both are cleanable, both handle temperatures far beyond polymers, and both come in stainless steel. The difference lies in what the porous skeleton is made of: packed powder particles in one case, a random mat of microscopic metal fibers in the other.

Construction: Particles vs Fibers

Powder sintered filter. Metal powder particles are packed and sintered into a monolithic porous body. Pores are the voids between roughly spherical particles, connected through discrete pore throats. The structure is regular and isotropic, and the element is one self-supporting piece of porous metal.

Metal fiber felt. Extremely fine metal fibers — far thinner than wires — are formed into a non-woven mat, then sintered so the crossing fibers bond at contact points. The result resembles metallic paper: a sheet of porous metal fabric that must usually be pleated and supported in a cartridge hardware, though self-supporting shapes are also produced.

Both achieve depth filtration, but the fiber mat contains far more pore surface area per unit volume. That single structural difference drives most of the performance contrast.

Filtration Performance

Retention range. Fiber felts reach finer filtration ratings than powder sintered media. The fine fiber mat presents a dense tangle of interception sites, enabling absolute ratings well into the fine-micron range. Powder sintered media covers the coarser-to-mid range of metal filtration ratings, overlapping felt at its fine end and extending far above it.

Permeability. At the same rating, a fiber felt is typically more restrictive than powder sintered media — more surface area means more drag. Conversely, at the same pressure-drop budget, a powder sintered element generally flows more freely. Where gas flow capacity or low energy consumption dominates, powder media often wins; where the finest possible metal-medium retention dominates, felt often wins.

Dirt release. Powder sintered structures tend to release captured contamination very cleanly during backpulsing because their smooth pore walls and large pore throats offer little for particles to snag on. Fiber mats can trap fines deep within the fiber tangle, which may gradually blind areas that backwashing cannot recover — a slow permeability decay over many cleaning cycles.

Mechanical and Thermal Behavior

Strength. This is powder sintered media’s clearest advantage. A sintered powder body is a metal component with structural section thickness: it withstands high differential pressure, vibration, and mechanical handling as a matter of course, and it can be machined, threaded, and welded like metal. Fiber felt is a sheet: its pressure capability comes from pleating area and support hardware, and its fibers — though individually strong metal — can fatigue, break, and compact under severe pulsation or rough handling. Fiber shedding under extreme stress is a known failure mode; a monolithic powder body has nothing to shed.

Thermal cycling. Both tolerate high temperatures, but massive monolithic elements absorb and survive thermal shock and steep gradients better than thin felts bonded into cartridges, where differential expansion between felt, end caps, and hardware stresses the bonds.

Geometry. Powder sintering makes the element itself: tubes, discs, cups, spargers, and custom shapes in one piece. Fiber felt starts as sheet, so it naturally becomes pleated cartridges and layered discs — excellent for maximizing area in a housing, less suited to self-supporting odd geometries.

Selection Guidance

Choose metal fiber felt when the application demands the finest retention available in an all-metal medium, high filtration area in a compact cartridge, or polishing duties where the element sees modest mechanical stress.

Choose powder sintered when the element must be a structural part — high differential pressure, pulsating flow, vibration, thermal shock — when the shape is tubular or custom, when sparging is involved, or when decades of clean/regenerate cycles make long-term permeability stability valuable.
