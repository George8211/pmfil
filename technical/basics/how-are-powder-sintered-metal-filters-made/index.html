How Are Powder Sintered Metal Filters Made?

2026-09-03  · 4 min read

Every powder sintered metal filter — whether a stainless steel cartridge or a titanium sparger — is made by the same fundamental route of powder metallurgy: select powder, form it into shape, then sinter it so the particles bond into a rigid porous body. Understanding these steps explains almost everything about how the filters behave in service.

Step 1: Powder Selection

Manufacturing starts with the metal powder itself. The powder’s particle size distribution is the primary control over the final pore structure: a bed of coarse particles leaves large voids; fine particles pack into smaller ones. Producers therefore choose and often blend powders to hit a target pore size and permeability.

Powder is also chosen for chemistry — stainless steel grades such as 316L are common for their corrosion resistance and sintering behavior, while titanium powder delivers the corrosion performance of titanium in porous form. Powder quality (purity, particle shape, flowability) matters: irregular, well-packed particles sinter into stronger, more uniform porous bodies.

Step 2: Forming the Green Compact

The powder is formed into the shape of the finished part while still loose or lightly bonded — the “green” compact. Common forming routes include:

  • Die compaction — powder is pressed in a rigid die at high pressure, giving accurate dimensions and good green strength. Typical for discs and small parts.
  • Isostatic pressing — the powder, sealed in a flexible mold, is compacted evenly from all directions by fluid pressure. This produces uniform density in tubes and cylinders and avoids the density gradients of uniaxial pressing.
  • Fill-and-sinter in the mold — the powder is simply filled into a shaped mold (sometimes around a mandrel for tubes) and sintered in place. The mold defines the near-net shape.

At this stage the part is fragile: the particles touch, but nothing bonds them yet.

Step 3: Sintering — Powder Metallurgy’s Core Process

Sintering is a heat treatment below the metal’s melting point. The compact is heated in a controlled atmosphere furnace — vacuum or inert/protective gas, and for titanium essentially always high vacuum or inert gas because of its reactivity — to a temperature where atoms become mobile at particle contact points.

Two things happen, and the balance between them defines the product:

  1. Neck growth. At each contact point, atoms diffuse and build a “neck” bridging the two particles. Necks grow until neighboring particles are fused into a continuous three-dimensional skeleton. This is what turns loose powder into a rigid, load-bearing part.
  2. Densification and pore rounding. Surface energy drives the pore network to shrink and smooth. Given enough time and temperature, pores would close entirely and the part would become solid metal. Sintered filters are deliberately under-sintered — held for the time needed for strong bonds but stopped while a controlled, open porosity remains.

This trade-off is the central craft of the process: more sintering means more strength and lower porosity; less means higher permeability but a weaker body. Filtration-grade products sit at a carefully controlled point in between, and small changes in time, temperature, or atmosphere shift pore size, strength, and permeability together. That is why different suppliers’ products of “the same” nominal rating can behave differently, and why verified specifications matter.

Step 4: Cooling and Atmosphere Control

As the part cools, atmosphere control remains critical. For stainless steel, the sintering atmosphere (often hydrogen-based or vacuum) manages carbon and oxygen levels to preserve corrosion resistance. For titanium, any exposure to oxygen or nitrogen at temperature would embrittle the surface, so vacuum integrity through the whole cycle is essential. The passive oxide film that protects finished titanium parts forms naturally afterward, on cooling and first exposure to air.

Step 5: Sizing, Machining, and Fitting

After sintering, the porous body is near its final geometry but may receive secondary operations:

  • Sizing or coining — light re-pressing to hit tight dimensional tolerances.
  • Machining — faces, threads, or sealing surfaces can be machined where a dense, pore-free surface is required. Machining porous material demands care to avoid smearing pores shut at the surface.
  • Welding and assembly — tubes are welded to end caps, collars, or threaded fittings; discs may be welded into rings or carriers. Welding porous sintered metal requires procedures that avoid collapsing the pore structure in the heat-affected zone.
  • Cleaning and verification — finished elements are cleaned of process residues and checked. Common verification methods include bubble point testing (which links pore size to a measurable pressure), permeability measurement, and rating verification.

How Processing Shapes Performance

Because pore size derives from powder size and sintering conditions, the same base material can be produced across a range of ratings: coarser powders, less densification. Filtration rating, porosity, and permeability are therefore not independent choices — they move together along the process curve, and mechanical strength moves with them in the opposite direction. Good filter selection respects these couplings rather than treating each parameter independently.
