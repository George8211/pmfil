Sintered Metal Filter vs Wire Mesh Filter

2026-09-07  · 3 min read

Both sintered metal filters and wire mesh filters are all-metal, cleanable, high-temperature media — which is exactly why they are so often compared, and so often confused. They differ fundamentally in how they are built and therefore how they filter: a mesh filter separates at a geometric screen surface; a powder sintered filter separates throughout a porous volume.

Construction: Screen vs Volume

Wire mesh filter. Woven from metal wires (often stainless steel), usually layered and sometimes sintered together to bond the plies into a rigid laminate. Filtration occurs at the plane where the fluid meets the weave: particles larger than the mesh openings stop at the surface. This is surface filtration.

Powder sintered filter. Made by sintering metal powder into a monolithic porous body, as described in our article on how these filters are made. Particles are captured throughout the wall thickness in the tortuous pore network. This is depth filtration.

The distinction sounds academic until you look at what it means in service.

Dirt-Holding Capacity

A surface screen has one plane of retention. Once its surface cakes over, flow drops sharply and cleaning is required. A depth structure spreads retained particles through a volume, so a sintered element of the same face area typically holds more contamination before reaching the same pressure-drop endpoint — and often releases it more completely during backwashing because there is no weave geometry for particles to lodge in permanently.

For duties with significant particulate load, depth filtration is usually the more forgiving choice. For duties protecting against occasional coarse debris — where the filter is more of a screen than a separator — mesh may be entirely sufficient and cheaper.

Retention Behavior and Rating

Mesh is defined by wire diameter and weave count, so its opening size is geometric and consistent: a 100-micron mesh presents fairly uniform 100-micron apertures. Mesh filters are easy to specify and their absolute cutoff at the aperture size is well understood — though very fine weaves become fragile and expensive below roughly the tens-of-microns range.

A sintered pore network is statistical: tortuous paths with varying cross-sections. Retention is expressed as a filtration rating (nominal or absolute, in micrometers) that describes particle capture efficiency rather than a single geometric opening. Sintered powder media can be produced at retention levels finer than practical wire weaves, extending all-metal filtration into the fine-particle range.

Strength and Fabrication

Both media types are strong compared with polymers, but in different ways. Sintered wire mesh laminates gain rigidity from bonded plies and are excellent as flat screens, discs, and screen packs. Powder sintered bodies are monolithic and shapeable into tubes, cups, and complex forms with integral connections — and their strength does not depend on keeping delicate wires intact. In abrasive, erosive, or high-pulsation service, a monolithic porous body has no weave that can unravel or a ply that can separate.

Cleaning and Regeneration

Both are cleanable, but with different behaviors:

  • Mesh backwashes well when the captured material is surface-held; fine particles driven into a weave can be difficult to remove completely.
  • Sintered powder media release depth-held contamination well under backpulse or ultrasonic cleaning, and tolerate aggressive chemical cleaning when process residues demand it.

Which Should You Choose?

Choose wire mesh when you need a geometric screen for coarse, known-size particles, a flat disc or screen pack, or the lowest-cost metal media for mild duties.

Choose powder sintered when the duty involves fine retention, significant dirt load, erosive conditions, complex element shapes, or functions beyond filtration — like sparging — that exploit a three-dimensional pore structure.

Many plants use both: mesh screens upstream as cheap guardians, sintered elements downstream for the real separation work.
