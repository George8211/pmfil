Stainless Steel Sintered Filters: Materials and Applications

2026-09-11  · 4 min read

Stainless steel is the workhorse material of sintered metal filtration. The majority of powder sintered filter elements, discs, and spargers in industrial service are stainless steel — not by habit, but because it combines the properties this technology needs most: corrosion resistance, sinterability, strength, broad availability, and reasonable cost.

Why Stainless Steel Suits Powder Sintering

Not every corrosion-resistant alloy sinters well. Powder metallurgy needs a material that forms strong interparticle bonds at practical sintering temperatures without melting, keeps its corrosion resistance through the thermal cycle, and is available as clean, consistent powder. Austenitic stainless steels — above all the 300 series — meet these requirements exceptionally well, which is why grades such as 316L and 304 dominate the field.

Sintering does modify the material in ways worth understanding:

  • Corrosion resistance is preserved but must be managed. A properly sintered stainless body, produced under the right atmosphere, retains the passive chromium oxide layer that makes stainless “stainless.” Poor atmosphere control can sensitize the material or leave free iron on surfaces, degrading corrosion performance — one reason qualified producers control furnace chemistry carefully and passivate finished parts.
  • Properties shift slightly from wrought values. Porous sintered stainless has lower apparent strength and thermal/electrical conductivity than solid wrought stainless of the same grade, because part of its volume is void. Design values for sintered parts are therefore established per product, not copied from wrought data sheets.
  • Composition can be tuned for sintering. The “L” grades (low carbon) exist partly to avoid sensitization during thermal processing — exactly the kind of exposure sintering involves.

Common Grades and What Drives the Choice

316L

The default choice for demanding duties. Its molybdenum content raises resistance to chlorides and reducing acids relative to 304, and the low carbon limit protects against sensitization during sintering and welding. For process filters exposed to mildly corrosive chemistry — many chemical, pharmaceutical, and food streams — 316L is the starting point and often the finish line. See our dedicated article: What Is a Sintered 316L Filter?

304

The economical austenitic. Slightly lower corrosion resistance than 316L (no molybdenum) but broadly adequate for water, steam, air, oils, and many food-grade duties. Where the chemistry is benign, 304 delivers the same sintered-filter mechanics at lower material cost.

Other Grades

Higher-alloy austenitics, duplex grades, and specialty stainless powders appear where duties outrun 316L — hot acids, high chloride, sulfur-bearing streams. Availability in powder form is narrower for these grades, and each requires its own sintering window; confirm grade availability and data with the supplier rather than assuming equivalence with wrought versions.

What Sintered Stainless Filters Do Well

The material and the structure multiply each other’s strengths:

  1. Temperature range. All-stainless construction tolerates steam, hot gases, and thermal cleaning cycles that destroy polymer media. The practical ceiling is set by the grade and atmosphere, far above any polymer’s.
  2. Cleanability and reuse. Stainless survives the aggressive cleaning methods — hot caustic, solvents, steam, ultrasonic — needed to regenerate a plugged element, supporting the reuse economics that justify metal media.
  3. Mechanical robustness. The sintered body resists pressure differential, vibration, and erosion in slurries and particle-laden flows.
  4. Regulatory familiarity. Austenitic stainless is the default “safe” wetted material in food and pharmaceutical plants, easing validation compared with exotic alloys.

Typical Applications

  • Chemical processing — liquid and gas filtration, catalyst retention, resin trap duty.
  • Pharmaceutical — process air and gas filtration, vent filtration, product-contact duties validated around 316L wetted surfaces.
  • Food and beverage — steam filtration, tank venting, cooking and CIP-compatible filtration where stainless is already the plant standard.
  • Polymers — melt filtration and viscous fluid processing at temperatures where only metal survives.
  • General industrial — compressor and instrument protection, hydraulic and lube oil conditioning, pneumatic system filtration.

Limits to Respect

Stainless sintered filters are not universal. Chloride stress-corrosion cracking sets a practical boundary for standard austenitics in hot, concentrated chloride service; highly reducing or oxidizing chemistries may demand a different alloy entirely; and the finest corrosion resistance requirements sometimes push beyond stainless to titanium or nickel alloys. Matching grade to chemistry — with temperature and concentration both considered — is the core of material selection. Summary

Stainless steel — most often 316L, frequently 304 — is the default sintered filter material because it sinters reliably, resists a wide swath of industrial chemistry, tolerates heat and aggressive cleaning, and costs less than the alternatives. Specify the grade from the fluid chemistry and temperature, and the sintered structure handles the mechanics.
